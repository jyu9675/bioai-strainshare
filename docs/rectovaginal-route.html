01

The premise is establishedfor pathogens, not commensals

That bacteria travel from the gut to the vagina is not a fringe idea — it is mainstream obstetrics. The gastrointestinal tract is the recognised reservoir for vaginal Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae); GBS lives in the lower bowel and seeds the vagina. That is precisely why every pregnant person receives a recto-vaginal swab near term and antibiotics in labour if positive.

E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae follow the same rectum→vagina/urethra path — a major driver of UTIs and neonatal sepsis. Several BV-associated taxa (Gardnerella, Prevotella, Sneathia, Fannyhessea) are implicated in cross-site movement too.

And the link is not merely anatomical. Culture the isolates and genotype them, and the same strain appears at both sites: for E. coli, about 85% of a woman’s matched vaginal and urinary isolates are one strain; and in late pregnancy the gut and vaginal communities actively converge — 30–60% of vaginal bacteria overlap with faecal.

Two further lines point the same way. In late pregnancy, rectal GBS carriage is the single strongest predictor of vaginal colonisation — the vagina acquires it from the rectum. And the gut is a persistent reservoir: intrapartum antibiotics crash vaginal GBS up to 10,000-fold within hours, yet the organism survives in the bowel and typically re-seeds the vagina afterwards. Clear the vagina, and the gut refills it. The route is real, and directional; what remains is resolving it in situ, across the whole community, over time.

Same strain, both sites

18/19 GBS-colonised women carried an identical GBS genotype in the rectum and the vagina.

Established by isolate genotyping — the reservoir and the vaginal strain are demonstrably the same organism. What’s missing is the metagenomic, directional, longitudinal version — and the outcomes and hygiene links.

02

The gap is strain-level proofwhat has, and has not, been shown

So for the pathogens, the same-strain question is already answered at the culture level — the isolate concordance is real. What is missing is the decisive in-situ observation: the same strain moving rectum→vagina within one woman across pregnancy, resolved directly from metagenomes across the whole community, with the direction and timing established, and linked to infection and to hygiene and access — especially where screening and treatment are hardest to reach.

The one directly relevant study is sobering in the right way. Goltsman et al. (2018) found that a pregnant woman’s gut carried the same Lactobacillus species that dominated her vagina — but the within-host strains were divergent, not identical. No study has yet confirmed an identical gut↔vaginal strain. That is the gap this proposal fills — honestly.

No gut reservoir of the commensals. The lesson: the question must target pathogens and opportunists, not the commensal community.

Hypothesis. In pregnancy, specific rectal bacteria (GBS, E. coli, BV-associated taxa) seed the vagina as the same strain, contributing to colonisation and infection; transfer is more frequent under limited hygiene and healthcare access.

Link sharing to outcomes

Associate cross-site strain sharing with vaginal infection and dysbiosis (BV, GBS status, symptomatic infection) and with preterm birth.

Test the access covariates

Model transmission against water, sanitation, hygiene and antenatal-care access to find who is most at risk — and the actionable points for prevention.

04

Design & target organismslongitudinal, paired, deep — and pointed at the right bugs

How the study is built

  • Population. Pregnant women in low-resource settings, with documented water/sanitation/hygiene and healthcare-access variation — existing East African (Kenya, Rwanda) and South African pregnancy-microbiome cohorts are natural partners or comparators.
  • Sampling. Paired rectal + vaginal swabs at each visit across pregnancy — longitudinal is what makes direction knowable.
  • Depth. Deep shotgun to ≥10× breadth on candidate taxa — the empirical strain-call floor from our benchmarks.
  • Controls. Per-plate negatives so whole-community contamination, which mimics sharing, can be flagged and removed.
  • Reference. A broad vaginal + gut catalogue plus per-sample assembled genomes — reference breadth is the sensitivity lever.

06

What it changesevidence for clinicians and policy — not prescriptions

The stakes are concentrated where the study looks: bacterial vaginosis and these cross-site organisms raise the risk of preterm birth and low birthweight, and that burden falls hardest on low- and middle-income settings with the least screening. The outputs then feed levers that already exist. GBS intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis is standard of care — this study says which women and which strains matter most, and whether screening should reach earlier or into the rectal reservoir directly. WASH interventions — clean water, sanitation — become testable structural preventions where transfer is highest. Microbiome-directed approaches such as Lactobacillus live biotherapeutics remain research-stage — though an oral probiotic (L. rhamnosus GR-1 / L. reuteri RC-14) already reduced both rectal and vaginal GBS in a randomised trial, a direct test of the reservoir model (a 2026 meta-analysis finds a directional GBS benefit, no clear BV effect) — and the Kwon Lab’s own domain.

The deliverable

A ranked, contamination-controlled list of bacteria that demonstrably move rectum→vagina in pregnancy — with direction, persistence, outcome links, and the risk factors that govern them.

§

On honesty

This proposal exists because the phenomenon it studies happened to a real person. That experience is proof at the clinical level — the level that mattered for her health. It does not need sequencing data to be true.

What the study can do is generate the strain-level, population-scale evidence that turns one lived case into a defensible, fundable finding. It will report what the data show — positive or negative — and it will not relabel a commensal result, or a trace at one site, as proof of pathogen transmission. A clean negative is itself worth publishing: it says the reservoir is elsewhere, or the depth insufficient, and points to the next study. All treatment decisions belong to a clinician; this work produces the evidence, not the prescription.

Limitations held in view: confounding by shared environment and sexual activity; the need for deep sequencing and a comprehensive reference; and the ethics of working with vulnerable, low-resource, pregnant participants — IRB approval, informed consent, and clinician-led management of anything found.
