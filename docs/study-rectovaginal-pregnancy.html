01

The premise is establishedfor pathogens, not commensals

That bacteria travel from the gut to the vagina is not a fringe idea — it is mainstream obstetrics. The gastrointestinal tract is the recognised reservoir for vaginal Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae); GBS lives in the lower bowel and seeds the vagina. That is precisely why every pregnant person receives a recto-vaginal swab near term and antibiotics in labour if positive.

E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae follow the same rectum→vagina/urethra path — a major driver of UTIs and neonatal sepsis. Several BV-associated taxa (Gardnerella, Prevotella, Sneathia, Fannyhessea) are implicated in cross-site movement too. The route is real; the open question is which organisms travel as the same strain, and in whom.

Why it matters in pregnancy

A colonised mother, an under-screened setting, and a vertical-transmission rate up to 50% turn a gut organism into a neonatal risk.

Bacterial vaginosis — the dysbiotic state these taxa create — raises the risk of preterm birth and low birthweight, outcomes that fall hardest on low- and middle-income settings.

02

The gap is strain-level proofwhat has, and has not, been shown

Rectovaginal colonisation is well documented by culture and PCR, and pathogen isolate genomes exist — including from low-resource cohorts. What is missing is the decisive observation: the same strain, demonstrated moving rectum→vagina within one woman across pregnancy, by strain-resolved metagenomics, and linked to infection and to hygiene and access.

The one directly relevant study is sobering in the right way. Goltsman et al. (2018) found that a pregnant woman’s gut carried the same Lactobacillus species that dominated her vagina — but the within-host strains were divergent, not identical. No study has yet confirmed an identical gut↔vaginal strain. That is the gap this proposal fills — honestly.

Our own pilot sharpens it further, and in a way that changes the design. Three women, deep paired rectal + vaginal shotgun, a 33-genome reference containing both the vaginal community and the transmissible pathogens, produced two different kinds of negative — and only one of them is a true absence. Telling them apart is what sets the target list and the sequencing depth below.

Our own pilot — two negatives, not one

1

A true absence. Gardnerella reached 185× vaginally at 0.96 breadth — and 0× in the rectum. Not one read, in any of the three women, for Gardnerella or Lactobacillus.

The commensals have no gut reservoir. Sequencing deeper will not find one.

2

A sub-threshold signal. The BV-associated taxa were at both sites in 3/3 women — P. bivia rectally at 6.7–11.4×, P. amnii rectum 3.8× → vagina 198×, P. disiens rectum 19.5× ↔ vagina 6.5×.

But overlapping breadth peaked near 0.15, under the 0.5 floor — so not one within-woman cross-site pair yielded a comparable genome. Co-detected, uncallable.

The commensals are out — including them only dilutes the signal. The BV taxa are in, and the pilot says exactly why the study was never going to call them: depth, not absence.

Hypothesis. In pregnancy, specific rectal bacteria (GBS, E. coli, BV-associated taxa) seed the vagina as the same strain, contributing to colonisation and infection; transfer is more frequent under limited hygiene and healthcare access.

Does it happen?

Detect within-woman shared strains

Rectum↔vagina strain identity by inStrain popANI ≥ 0.999 on ≥50% breadth, contamination-controlled, focused on GBS / E. coli / BV taxa.

2

Which way, which bugs?

Infer direction and rank taxa

Use longitudinal sampling to establish rectum-before-vagina timing, then rank organisms by transmission rate, directionality, and persistence.

3

Does it cause disease?

Link sharing to outcomes

Associate cross-site strain sharing with vaginal infection and dysbiosis (BV, GBS status, symptomatic infection) and with preterm birth.

4

Who is at risk, what helps?

Test the access covariates

Model transmission against water, sanitation, hygiene and antenatal-care access to find who is most at risk — and the actionable points for prevention.

04

Design & target organismslongitudinal, paired, deep — and pointed at the right bugs

How the study is built

  • Population. Pregnant women in low-resource settings, with documented water/sanitation/hygiene and healthcare-access variation.
  • Sampling. Paired rectal + vaginal swabs at each visit across pregnancy — longitudinal is what makes direction knowable.
  • Depth. Deep shotgun to ≥10× breadth on candidate taxa — the empirical strain-call floor from our benchmarks, and the exact thing the pilot’s BV co-detections fell short of.
  • Controls. Per-plate negatives so whole-community contamination, which mimics sharing, can be flagged and removed.
  • Reference. A broad vaginal + gut catalogue plus per-sample assembled genomes — reference breadth is the sensitivity lever.

Analysis runs through strainshare: shared-strain calls against a within- vs between-person null, a contamination discriminator, a generalist filter, and longitudinal direction inference.

What “same strain” means, quantitatively

The 0.999 cutoff is not a convention borrowed from the literature — it was calibrated against known divergence. Reads simulated from three real genomes (L. crispatus, L. iners, G. vaginalis) across four mutation rates × three replicates gave 39 real inStrain comparisons at 20×. popANI tracks 1−divergence with SD < 1e−4, and all three species curves overlap — the resolution limit is species-agnostic.

So a “shared strain” in this study is a defined quantity, not a threshold assertion: two populations that have diverged by less than roughly 0.1%. Below 0.05% the call is unambiguous; at 0.1% it is a knife edge; at 0.2% and beyond the strains separate cleanly. Every transmission claim the study makes will carry that resolution with it.

06

What it changesevidence for clinicians and policy — not prescriptions

The outputs feed levers that already exist. GBS intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis is standard of care — this study says which women and which strains matter most, and whether screening should reach earlier or into the rectal reservoir directly. WASH interventions — clean water, sanitation — become testable structural preventions where transfer is highest. Microbiome-directed approaches such as Lactobacillus live biotherapeutics remain research-stage, and the Kwon Lab’s own domain.

The deliverable

A ranked, contamination-controlled list of bacteria that demonstrably move rectum→vagina in pregnancy — with direction, persistence, outcome links, and the risk factors that govern them.

§

On honesty

This proposal exists because the phenomenon it studies happened to a real person. That experience is proof at the clinical level — the level that mattered for her health. It does not need sequencing data to be true.

What the study can do is generate the strain-level, population-scale evidence that turns one lived case into a defensible, fundable finding. It will report what the data show — positive or negative — and it will not relabel a commensal result, or a trace at one site, as proof of pathogen transmission. A clean negative is itself worth publishing: it says the reservoir is elsewhere, or the depth insufficient, and points to the next study. The pilot above is exactly that distinction drawn on our own data — one true absence, one sub-threshold signal, reported as two different things. All treatment decisions belong to a clinician; this work produces the evidence, not the prescription.

Limitations held in view: confounding by shared environment and sexual activity; the need for deep sequencing and a comprehensive reference; and the ethics of working with vulnerable, low-resource, pregnant participants — IRB approval, informed consent, and clinician-led management of anything found.
